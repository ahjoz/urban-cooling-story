Hotter Cities, Unequal Pressure

How rising cooling demand and longer heatwaves are reshaping local adaptation priorities

As global temperatures rise, cities will require more cooling, but the scale and type of pressure will vary widely.

This story compares projected annual cooling degree days and heatwave duration across approximately 1,000 cities under 1.5°C, 2°C, and 3°C warming scenarios.

Cooling degree days (CDD) measure accumulated warmth above 21°C or approximately 70°F. Higher CDD values indicate greater potential need for cooling.

Rising temperatures will increase urban cooling needs

As global warming increases from 1.5°C to 3°C, average annual cooling demand across the cities in the dataset rises from about 1,149 to 1,325 cooling degree days, an increase of roughly 176 °C-days. This suggests that even relatively small increases in global warming can translate into substantially greater cooling needs for cities.

Regional cooling demand rises unevenly

Cooling demand rises across every region, but regions begin from very different baselines. South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa remain among the highest-demand regions across warming scenarios, while Europe & Central Asia starts from a much lower level.

Cities face different types of cooling pressure

Some cities already face high cooling demand, while others may experience sharper future increases. Comparing both dimensions reveals compounding, emerging, persistent, and lower-relative pressure.

Some cities may face both rising cooling demand and longer heatwaves

Cooling demand captures accumulated warmth, but it does not show how long extreme heat conditions may persist.

Different risk patterns call for different local responses

These projections identify patterns of climate exposure, not a complete measure of vulnerability. Local decisions should also consider infrastructure capacity, population vulnerability, cooling access, and available resources.

Emerging Pressure

Jember, Indonesia

Current cooling demand may be more moderate, but projected increases point to growing future pressure.

Local Response

Early codes + future energy planning

Use building standards, passive cooling strategies, and long-term energy planning before higher cooling demand becomes more difficult and costly to manage.

Compounding Pressure

Bamako, Mali

High existing cooling demand combines with strong future growth, creating multiple layers of heat pressure.

Local Response

Grid capacity + equitable cooling

Strengthen energy infrastructure while expanding reliable and affordable access to cooling for the most heat-exposed populations.

Lower Relative Pressure

Berlin, Germany

Cooling demand remains lower relative to hotter cities, but future warming can still introduce new challenges.

Local Response

Monitoring + preventive planning

Track changing heat conditions and invest early in passive cooling, shade, building adaptation, and heat-response planning.

Persistent Pressure

Karachi, Pakistan

Cooling demand is already high and is likely to remain a significant challenge as temperatures continue to rise.

Local Response

Efficiency + affordability

Improve building and cooling efficiency while addressing affordability so that higher energy needs do not deepen existing inequalities.

The Takeaway

There is no one-size-fits-all cooling strategy. Cities need responses that reflect both their current burden and the pressures emerging under higher warming.
