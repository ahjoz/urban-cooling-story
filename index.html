Hotter Cities, Unequal Pressure

How rising cooling demand and longer heatwaves are reshaping local adaptation priorities

As global temperatures rise, cities will require more cooling, but the scale and type of pressure will vary widely.

This story compares projected annual cooling degree days and heatwave duration across approximately 1,000 cities under 1.5°C, 2°C, and 3°C warming scenarios.

Cooling degree days (CDD)

measure how much and for how long temperatures exceed 21°C, or approximately 70°F.

Rising temperatures will increase urban cooling needs

As global warming increases from 1.5°C to 3°C, cities are projected to require more cooling. Across the cities in the dataset, average annual cooling degree days rise between the scenarios.

Average cooling demand across cities rises as global warming increases from 1.5°C to 3°C

Cooling demand rises unevenly across regions

Cooling demand rises in every region, but the starting levels differ dramatically. Regions with higher baselines may face sustained pressure, while others may experience faster growth.

Regional averages reveal broad patterns, but they can hide important differences between individual cities.

Regions begin from very different cooling baselines

Where cooling demand grows most

Looking at the increase from 1.5°C to 3°C makes it easier to see which regions face the largest additional cooling demand.

Current demand and future growth reveal four types of cooling pressure

Cities face different types of cooling pressure

Some cities already face high cooling demand, while others may experience sharper future increases. Comparing both dimensions reveals compounding, emerging, persistent, and lower-relative pressure.

Cities are grouped relative to the dataset medians for existing cooling demand and projected increase.

The four categories describe broad patterns, but selected cities show what these trajectories look like in practice.

The same warming level creates very different city trajectories

Selected cities show how annual cooling demand changes between 1.5°C and 3°C warming.

Some begin with high demand, while others experience sharper increases under higher warming.

Some cities may face both rising cooling demand and longer heatwaves

Cooling demand captures accumulated warmth, but it does not show how long extreme heat conditions may persist.

By increases in cooling demand and heatwave duration reveal where multiple pressures may intensify together.

The cities that may feel rising heat most are not defined by one measure alone. Effective planning must reflect both current cooling burdens and the pressures emerging under higher warming.

Different risk patterns call for different local responses

These projections identify patterns of climate exposure, not a complete measure of vulnerability. Local decisions should also consider infrastructure capacity, population vulnerability, cooling access, and available resources.

The takeaway

There is no one-size-fits-all cooling strategy. Cities need responses that reflect both their current burden and the pressures emerging under higher warming.
